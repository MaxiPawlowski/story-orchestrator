## E01 (place): Gullwater Locks

Keys: Gullwater Locks, old river locks

{{// so:protect}}
Gullwater Locks are the old river locks two miles upstream from the town's barge landing, where the river climbs out of the salt-marsh lowlands. Two stone chambers and a narrow central island connect the lower town reach to the upper river.

From town, a towpath along the east bank reaches the lower gates in about forty minutes on foot. Barges approach from downstream along the town reach or from upstream through a short, straight cut. A stepped service path on the west bank leads from the lower landing to the upper gate platform. Footbridges across the gate heads connect both banks with the central island; they are narrow and unsuitable for carts. The east-bank towpath continues upstream beyond the upper chamber, while the west-bank path ends at the upper platform.

Boats must pass through both chambers to travel between the upper and lower reaches. The hand-operated gates cannot be opened against unequal water levels; filling or draining a chamber takes time, and only one river barge fits in each chamber. Pedestrians can leave by either bank at the lower gates or follow the east-bank towpath upriver.

Current state: The stonework is weathered and the gate mechanisms require slow, manual operation. The towpath and footbridges remain passable; no boat has priority of passage.

## E02 (item): Ferryman's Tally

Keys: Ferryman's Tally, ferryman’s tally, notched stick

{{// so:protect}}
The Ferryman's Tally is a wooden stick used to record and settle river debts. When a ferryman and a debtor agree on a sum for passage or freight, they cut a matching notch into the tally, then split the stick lengthwise through the marks. Each keeps one half. To settle the debt, they fit the halves together, check the grain and matching notches, and mark the paid notches across both halves in each other's presence.
The tally proves only the debts recorded on it; it is not money, cannot compel payment, and does not establish ownership of a cargo. A lone half cannot demonstrate that a debt remains unpaid. Disputed marks require the holders to compare their halves and agree on the account. Ferrymen want the record preserved until payment; debtors want paid marks acknowledged so they cannot be charged twice.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: The tally is used along the river to reconcile passage and freight accounts. No particular holder, unpaid sum, or connection to the unsigned crate has been established.

## E03 (concept): Marsh Sight

Keys: Marsh Sight, reading the fog, read the fog

{{// so:protect}}
Marsh Sight is the knack some marsh locals develop for reading nearby fog through patient observation. They watch how mist streams, pools, lifts, and breaks against reeds and pilings, comparing those movements with the wind and tide. A practiced reader can sometimes distinguish an open water channel from a reed bed, notice a sheltered bank, or judge that something solid is disturbing the mist ahead. These are local, short-range inferences, not visions: the reader must actually see the fog's movement, and unfamiliar ground makes their judgment less reliable.
Marsh Sight cannot identify a person or cargo hidden in the mist, read thoughts, reveal ownership, predict the future, or map a whole marsh. A disturbance does not tell its cause with certainty; a post, boat, or bank may produce similar signs. Still fog offers few clues, while gusts, driving rain, and competing boat wakes can make a reading useless. It never guarantees safe passage or replaces soundings and channel markers.
Using the knack costs time and attention. A useful reading requires stopping to watch several changes in the fog; attempting it while steering or hurrying risks missing ordinary hazards. Prolonged watching strains the eyes and leaves the reader tired, making later readings less dependable. Practice improves judgment, but no local is infallible.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: Some locals use Marsh Sight as an aid to navigation around the river town's salt marsh. Any particular reading remains an interpretation of visible conditions, not established fact.

## E05 (history): The Drowning of Old Quay

Keys: Drowning of Old Quay, Old Quay, first harbour

{{// so:protect}}
The Drowning of Old Quay was the flood that destroyed the river town's first harbour. Prolonged rain swelled the river while a storm tide drove seawater inland through the salt marsh. With the river unable to empty, water overtopped the harbour bank, undermined the quay walls and swept through the low warehouses. Boats broke their moorings; collapsing masonry blocked the old landing channel. The surviving townspeople abandoned the flooded waterfront and established the working harbour on higher ground upriver.

Old Quay was not rebuilt. Its submerged foundations and silted channel remain downstream of the present landing, along the marsh edge. Low water exposes portions of the broken quay, but unstable mud and concealed masonry make it unsafe to land there. The disaster is remembered as a flood, not as evidence of a curse or deliberate sabotage. Stories about bells heard beneath the water are local folklore, not established fact.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: River trade uses the newer harbour. Old Quay remains an abandoned, partly submerged ruin; its former landing channel is unsuitable for cargo barges.

## E07 (concept): Tide Oath

Keys: Tide Oath, tide oath

{{// so:protect}}
The Tide Oath is a promise river traders swear while the incoming tide rises against the landing steps. With one hand dipped in the river, the trader names the cargo or bargain, the person to whom the promise is owed, and the agreed obligation, then says: "As this tide comes in, I hold my word until the bargain is answered."
The oath binds only the terms spoken aloud and accepted by the other party; it does not establish ownership of disputed cargo or oblige anyone to accept an unnamed debt. A witness repeats the terms so that neither trader can later claim a different bargain. If the tide has turned outward, the ceremony must wait for the next incoming tide.
Its force is communal, not magical. A trader who breaks the oath must make restitution to the injured party and publicly acknowledge the breach before witnesses. Until then, traders who know of the breach may refuse credit or demand payment in advance. The river neither punishes oathbreakers nor reveals whether a speaker is truthful.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: River traders at the landing recognize the Tide Oath as a solemn commercial promise; no particular oath or breach has yet been established.

## E16 (history): The Barge Strike

Keys: Barge Strike, barge strike, bargemen's strike, season without river trade

{{// so:protect}}
The Barge Strike lasted one trading season. The bargemen collectively refused to carry cargo after quay merchants imposed lower freight payments while leaving crews responsible for losses in transit. Barges remained moored and the town's regular river trade stopped; the river itself was neither dammed nor made impassable. Small private boats could still travel, but could not replace the barges' carrying capacity.

Salt accumulated in riverside stores, imported grain grew scarce, and traders paid more to move goods overland along the marsh edge. Crews supported one another from pooled provisions, while some households left to find work elsewhere. Trade resumed after the merchants and crew delegates agreed on freight rates and limits to crews' liability for spoiled cargo. Resumption did not erase the season's debts or restore every lost business.

What people say: Bargemen remember the stoppage as proof that the town depends on their labour; some merchant families call it a season of deliberate starvation. Stories credit a single brave captain or blame outside agitators. These are competing accounts, not established explanations: the stoppage involved crews acting together, and shortages had several causes. No concealed instigator or secret bargain is established in this shared history.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: River cargo service has resumed. The strike remains a point of dispute whenever freight rates, cargo liability or the price of staple goods are discussed.

{{// so:protect}}
The Tidewarden's Lantern is a hooded brass oil lamp carried by the salt-marsh guides who lead travelers between the river trading town and the marsh channels. Slotted shutters let its bearer show a narrow beam for checking footing or a broad glow for signaling another guide. A wire cage protects the glass from reeds and boat hooks; a carrying loop lets it hang from a skiff's bow.

The lamp burns ordinary lamp oil for about six hours on a full reservoir. Its flame must be lit and tended by hand. The hood shelters it from wind and spray, not immersion: a submerged lantern goes out, and wet wick must be dried or replaced. Its light reveals nearby stakes, mud edges, and exposed footing, but cannot see through thick fog, find submerged paths, predict the tide, or make an unsafe crossing safe. Shutter signals work only within sight and mean nothing to someone who has not learned the guides' code.

Marsh guides want these lamps for dependable light and signaling; ferrymen and reed cutters seek spare ones for night work. A lantern is equipment, not proof that its bearer is a trained guide or entitled to command a boat. Oil, replacement wick, and intact glass must be obtained in town.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: Working examples are carried by the marsh guides. No particular lantern has yet been established as lost, stolen, damaged, or in the player's possession.

## E28 (concept): Tide Oath

Keys: Tide Oath, tide oath, incoming tide

{{// so:protect}}
The Tide Oath is a public promise river traders swear while the incoming tide is visibly rising. Before at least one witness, each trader names the cargo or service, the person owed, the agreed payment, and the deadline, then says: "As this tide comes in, I will bring what I have promised." Each party must speak their own undertaking; silence does not bind anyone.
An oath obliges its speaker to deliver the named goods or service on the stated terms, disclose any known defect before acceptance, and notify the other party promptly if delivery becomes impossible. Changes or release require the affected parties' express agreement before a witness; another tide does not renew, erase, or alter a promise.
The oath has no magical power: it cannot detect lies, compel obedience, establish ownership, or make unsafe passage safe. It binds only consenting speakers and only the terms spoken, not their crews, relatives, or later buyers. Witnesses attest to what they heard, not whether a crate's contents or a trader's claims are genuine.
Its cost is public accountability. A trader who breaks an oath without securing release may lose credit, face demands for restitution, or be refused future business by those who hear credible testimony. Such consequences depend on people and evidence, not supernatural punishment or automatic town-wide enforcement. Swearing alone, or on an ebbing tide, may be an ordinary promise but is not a witnessed Tide Oath.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: River traders in the marsh-edge trading town recognize the Tide Oath as a custom of witnessed trade. No particular trader, cargo, or outstanding promise is established by this entry.

## E29 (history): The Barge Strike

Keys: Barge Strike, bargemen's strike

{{// so:protect}}
The Barge Strike was the season when the bargemen stopped commercial river traffic through the trading town at the edge of the salt marsh. They refused to move cargo until the quay merchants paid overdue wages and withdrew a new charge for tying up at the landing. Barges remained moored for a full trading season; crews kept the channel open for rescue boats, but carried no goods for sale.
Salt and other marsh cargo piled up in warehouses while upriver supplies grew scarce. The stoppage ended when the merchants paid the arrears and abandoned the mooring charge. River trade resumed, but the lost season left debts that the settlement did not erase. The stoppage and its terms are established history; claims that either side deliberately starved the town remain partisan accusations, not settled fact.

Current state: The river is open to trade again. The Barge Strike remains a reference point in arguments over wages, landing fees, and whether the merchants can be trusted to honor an agreement.

## E31 (history): The Barge Strike

Keys: Barge Strike, bargemen, river stoppage

{{// so:protect}}
The Barge Strike was the season when the town's bargemen collectively refused to move cargo along the river. They kept their barges tied up from the beginning of the trading season to its end, stopping river freight rather than physically damming the water. Foot travel and small private crossings remained possible, but neither could replace the barges' carrying capacity.

The stoppage began over reduced freight payments and charges imposed on crews for delays at the landing. With cargo stranded, merchants lost sales, warehouses filled, and marsh salt could not reach its usual buyers by river. Bargemen's households also went without their normal earnings. At the season's end, representatives of the crews and merchants agreed to restore the previous freight payments and to stop charging crews for delays they had not caused. The bargemen then resumed hauling.

Public accounts differ from these events in emphasis: merchants commonly call the strike a season of ruin caused by stubborn crews, while bargemen remember it as the season they proved that river trade depended on their labor. Neither account establishes that every merchant opposed the settlement or every bargeman supported the stoppage. Claims of secret sponsors or deliberate sabotage remain unverified and are not established history.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: River freight is moving again. The Barge Strike remains a public point of reference whenever freight payments or responsibility for landing delays are disputed.

## E32 (faction): Saltmen's Guild

Keys: Saltmen's Guild, Saltmen, marsh salt

{{// so:protect}}
The Saltmen's Guild is a local association of several dozen salt cutters and carriers working the marsh beside the river trading town. Its crews cut salt crust from shallow drying beds, drain and dry it, then carry it in lined baskets to the landing for weighing and shipment. Rain interrupts cutting; high tides can flood the beds and make the carrying paths unsafe.
The guild maintains the paths between its beds and the landing, assigns crews, and records the weight and owner of each salt consignment. Buyers arrange collection with a tally keeper at the landing; disputed weights can be checked there before a load changes hands. Guild members can refuse to carry an unsafe or unrecorded load, but their authority covers their own labor and salt beds, not the town's other cargo or river traffic. An unfamiliar crate is not guild property merely because it arrives at their landing.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: The guild is cutting and carrying marsh salt for the town's river trade. Its tally records are the practical starting point for questions about a claimed guild salt shipment.

## E34 (faction): Reed Wardens — Town Marsh-Path Watch

Keys: Reed Wardens, Reed Warden, marsh watch, marsh-path watch

{{// so:protect}}
The Reed Wardens are the town's marsh-path watch: twelve paid wardens led by a watch captain, usually working in pairs. Their reach covers the landing, the raised paths linking town to the salt marsh, and the marked crossings within half a day's walk. They have no standing patrols beyond those crossings and no authority over river traffic outside the town landing.

Wardens inspect path markers and footbridges, warn travelers of flooded crossings, escort stranded walkers to firm ground, and investigate theft or violence on the paths. They may close an unsafe crossing, question witnesses, and detain a suspected offender for delivery to the town watch. They cannot impose sentences, collect private debts, or seize cargo merely because its owner is absent. A disputed shipment requires evidence of an offense before they may hold it.

Their small numbers mean that not every path is watched at once. They rely on travelers' reports and local guides; fog, rising water, and damaged markers can defeat their patrols. A warden's badge is not a guarantee that a route remains safe.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: The Reed Wardens maintain paired patrols on the marked marsh paths and receive reports at the town landing. Their ordinary duties do not establish who owns an unclaimed crate.

## E35 (item): The Ferryman's Tally

Keys: Ferryman's Tally, ferryman’s tally, notched stick, river debt

{{// so:protect}}
The Ferryman's Tally is a wooden stick used to record and settle river debts for passage or carriage. The ferryman and debtor agree on the charge before a notch is cut; each notch represents one agreed unit of debt. A tally is split lengthwise so each party keeps a matching half. To settle, they fit the halves together, verify the grain and notches, and exchange the agreed payment. Both parties then mark the corresponding notches as paid on both halves.
The tally is evidence of an agreement, not magical compulsion or money: it cannot force payment, identify an unknown debtor, or prove a claim when its halves do not match. A lost half requires witnesses and a fresh agreement rather than an invented balance. Ferrymen want tallies honored so they can keep trading; debtors want their matching halves preserved so paid charges cannot be claimed twice.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: The Ferryman's Tally is a recognized river-trade practice. No particular holder, unpaid balance, or connection to the unsigned crate has been established.

## E36 (history): The Barge Strike

Keys: Barge Strike, bargemen, strike season

{{// so:protect}}
The Barge Strike was the season when the town's bargemen collectively refused to carry freight along the river. They demanded payment of overdue wages and an end to landing fees deducted from their pay. Barges remained moored at the landings; crews declined to load, tow, or pilot cargo. The river itself remained navigable, but the organized stoppage halted the town's bulk river trade for an entire trading season.
Salt piled up in stores near the marsh while grain and other upriver supplies became scarce and expensive. Small boats still carried people and modest loads, but could not replace the barges. Merchants eventually agreed to settle the wage arrears and publish landing fees before crews accepted a load. The bargemen then resumed work.
The stoppage and its settlement are established local history. Claims that every crew supported it, or that every merchant profited from the shortages, are partisan recollections rather than established facts.

## E37 (history): The Drowning of Old Quay

Keys: Drowning of Old Quay, Old Quay, first harbour, First Harbour Flood

{{// so:protect}}
The Drowning of Old Quay is the flood that destroyed the river trading town's first harbour, built on low ground beside the salt marsh. During a prolonged river rise, a high tide held the river back and water overtopped the harbour bank. The bank then breached, flooding the warehouses and houses behind the quay. Boats broke their moorings; sections of the quay collapsed as the water scoured away their foundations. People fled toward higher ground, but not everyone escaped. No reliable total of the dead survives.

After the water receded, silt filled the harbour basin and the broken bank left the site exposed to subsequent tides. The surviving traders established their landing farther upriver on higher ground rather than rebuilding the first harbour. Old Quay remained a name for the drowned site and the ruins at the marsh edge.

Local accounts differ from this established sequence. Some families tell of a warning bell sounding through the night; others say the bell fell silent before the bank broke. Boatmen sometimes attribute the flood to an offended river. These are remembered accounts and beliefs, not established causes or proof of supernatural intervention.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: The first harbour is abandoned. Fragments of quay wall and warehouse foundations emerge at low water, but shifting mud and tidal channels make the site unsafe to approach. The town uses its upriver landing; the Drowning remains a warning against treating a low tide as evidence that the river is tame.

## E38 (concept): Tide Oath

Keys: Tide Oath, tide oath, traders' promise

{{// so:protect}}
The Tide Oath is a public promise river traders swear on the incoming tide to deal honestly in a named shipment or voyage. Before witnesses, the trader touches the rising river with an open hand, names the cargo and the party owed its delivery or payment, and says: "As this water comes upriver, let my word come good."
The oath binds the swearer to disclose known damage or shortages, keep the agreed terms, and account openly for cargo or money entrusted to them. If flood, wreck, or another obstacle prevents fulfilment, they must notify the other party at the next safe landing and seek a witnessed settlement; swearing does not guarantee a safe voyage.
Its authority is customary, not magical: the tide neither detects lies nor punishes oathbreakers. Witnesses can attest only to the promise and what they actually saw. Traders who knowingly break it risk refused credit, lost customers, and exclusion from shared cargo arrangements by those who believe the evidence. These costs are not automatic or universal, and the oath grants no power to seize goods, compel testimony, or override town law. It binds only the person who freely swore it and only to the terms spoken; it cannot establish ownership of an unnamed crate or impose a debt on a stranger.
{{// so:end}}
Current state: River traders still swear the Tide Oath on the incoming tide. No particular trader, shipment, breach, or settlement is established by this entry.

## E39 (history): The Drowning of Old Quay

Keys: Drowning of Old Quay, Old Quay, first harbour

{{// so:protect}}
The Drowning of Old Quay is the flood that destroyed the river town's first harbour. A swollen river met a high incoming tide, backing water into the harbour faster than it could drain. Water overtopped the quay, scoured the ground behind its retaining walls, and brought sections of the waterfront down. Warehouses collapsed, moored boats broke loose, and people were killed or driven from their homes. The harbour basin filled with debris and silt; rebuilding on the same low ground was abandoned, and trade moved upriver to higher banks.

These events are established by surviving harbour accounts and the exposed remains of the quay. The number of dead is not securely recorded. Local accounts disagree about the warning: some families say a bell rang too late, while others say boat crews warned the waterfront before the walls fell. Marsh tales claim that a bell can still be heard beneath the floodwater. These are remembered accounts and folklore, not established explanations of the disaster.

The drowning left the old waterfront unsuitable for regular berthing. Low tide can expose broken masonry, but shifting silt and submerged timbers make salvage hazardous. Neither an exposed ruin nor a recovered object establishes who owned a present-day cargo.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: Old Quay remains an abandoned, tidal ruin. The working harbour is upriver; residents still invoke the drowning when arguing about flood precautions and the risks of building on low ground.

## E40 (place): Gullwater Locks

Keys: Gullwater Locks, Gullwater, old river locks

{{// so:protect}}
Gullwater Locks are the old river locks two miles upstream from the town landing, where the river climbs beyond the salt marsh. A pair of stone chambers links the lower tidal reach to the upper river. Boats enter through the downstream gates from town and leave through the upstream gates; passage requires filling or draining each chamber and opening its gates in sequence. The locks cannot pass a boat wider than their narrow stone chambers, and a closed gate is not a navigable shortcut.

On foot, the locks are reached by the towpath from the town landing, a walk of about forty minutes. The towpath continues upstream beyond the upper gates. Stone steps beside the downstream chamber connect the towpath to the lockside; a narrow footbridge across the lower gates leads to the opposite bank's maintenance path, which ends at the upstream sluice. Chamber walls are steep, and the steps are the ordinary way between the water's edge and the lockside.
{{// so:end}}

Current state: The old masonry and gates remain in place. The towpath and lockside steps provide access from town; boat passage depends on the gates and water levels being checked on arrival.
